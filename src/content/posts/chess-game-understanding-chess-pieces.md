Every chess game starts with the same 32 pieces: 16 white and 16 black. Each side has **one king, one queen, two rooks, two bishops, two knights and eight pawns**. Once you know how each one moves, and roughly what it's worth, you can start to plan.

## Setting up the board

Turn the board so that each player has a **light square in the bottom-right corner**. On the back row, from the corners in: rooks, knights, bishops. The queen goes on the square of her own colour and the king beside her. Pawns fill the row in front. White moves first.

## The king

The king moves **one square in any direction**. It's the most important piece: if your king is in check (under attack) and can't escape, that's **checkmate** and you lose. Your king can never move into check.

Early in the game, keep the king safe, usually by castling. In the endgame, when there are few pieces left, the king becomes a strong attacking piece.

## The queen (worth 9)

The queen moves **any number of squares in any direction**: up, down, sideways or diagonally. It's the most powerful piece on the board. Beginners often bring it out too early, where it gets chased around by smaller pieces. Develop your knights and bishops first.

## The rook (worth 5)

Rooks move **any number of squares in a straight line**, horizontally or vertically. They're strongest on **open files** (columns with no pawns) and on the opponent's second row. Two rooks working together on the same row or column are very powerful.

## The bishop (worth 3)

Bishops move **any number of squares diagonally**. Each bishop stays on the same colour all game, so you have one light-squared and one dark-squared bishop. A pair of bishops is often worth a little more than a bishop and a knight.

## The knight (worth 3)

The knight moves in an **L-shape**: two squares in a straight line and then one square to the side. It's the only piece that can **jump over other pieces**. Knights are strongest in the centre and are famous for **forks**, attacking two pieces at once.

## The pawn (worth 1)

Pawns move **one square forward**, or **two squares on their first move**. Unlike other pieces, they **capture diagonally**, one square forward to the left or right. Pawns can never move backwards.

## Special moves

### Castling

Once per game, you can move your king two squares toward a rook, and then move that rook to the square the king just crossed. You can only castle if:

- Neither the king nor that rook has moved yet,
- There are no pieces between them,
- Your king is not in check, and
- The king doesn't pass through or land on a square under attack.

Castling tucks your king into safety and brings your rook into play.

### En passant

If a pawn moves two squares forward from its starting square and lands right beside an enemy pawn, the enemy pawn can capture it **as if it had moved only one square**. This must be done immediately on the next move, or the chance is gone.

### Promotion

When a pawn reaches the far side of the board, it's **promoted** to a queen, rook, bishop or knight of the same colour. Most players choose a queen.

## Piece values at a glance

| Piece | Value | How it moves |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pawn | 1 | Forward one (two on first move), captures diagonally |
| Knight | 3 | L-shape, jumps over pieces |
| Bishop | 3 | Diagonally, any distance |
| Rook | 5 | Straight lines, any distance |
| Queen | 9 | Any direction, any distance |
| King | n/a | One square in any direction |

Use these values to decide whether a trade is worth it. Giving up a knight (3) to win a rook (5) is good business.

## Tips for beginners

- **Control the centre** with pawns and pieces early.
- **Develop** your knights and bishops before moving the same piece twice.
- **Castle early** to protect your king.
- **Before every move, check** what your opponent is threatening.
