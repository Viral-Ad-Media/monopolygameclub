Draughts, known as checkers in North America, is easy to learn in five minutes and takes years to master. This guide covers **English draughts**, the version most people in the English-speaking world play at home, and then shows you how to win.

## What you need

- An **8 × 8 board** of alternating light and dark squares
- **12 pieces** for each player, usually one dark set and one light set

## Setting up

Place the board so that each player has a **dark square in their bottom-left corner**. All the action happens on the dark squares. Each player places their 12 pieces on the dark squares of the three rows nearest them, leaving the two middle rows empty.

In English draughts, the player with the **dark pieces moves first**.

## How pieces move

An ordinary piece, called a **man**, moves **one square diagonally forward** onto an empty dark square. Men can never move backwards.

## Capturing

If an enemy piece is diagonally next to your piece and the square directly beyond it is empty, you can **jump over it** and remove it from the board.

- **Captures are compulsory.** If you can capture, you must. If you have more than one capture available, you choose which one to make.
- **Multiple jumps.** If, after jumping, your piece can jump again, it must keep going in the same turn. A chain of jumps can change direction.
- Men can only capture **forwards** in English draughts.

## Kings

When one of your men reaches the far row, it's **crowned** and becomes a king. Place a second piece on top to mark it. If a man is crowned during a capture, its turn ends there.

A king moves and captures **diagonally forwards and backwards**, one square at a time, which makes it far more powerful than a man.

## Winning

You win when your opponent **can't make a legal move**, either because all their pieces have been captured or because the pieces they have left are blocked in. If neither player can make progress, the game is a draw.

## Strategy tips

### Control the centre

Pieces in the middle of the board have more options and can support each other. Pieces on the edges can only move one way.

### Keep your back row as long as you can

Your back row stops your opponent from getting kings. Many players keep two back-row pieces in place until late in the game.

### Move in pairs

A piece with a friend behind it can't be jumped from the front, because there's no empty landing square. Advance your pieces together.

### Use forced captures against your opponent

Because captures are compulsory, you can offer one piece to force your opponent into a position where you take two or three back. This "shot" is the most important tactic in draughts.

### Trade when you're ahead

If you have more pieces, swap one for one. Every even trade makes your advantage bigger in proportion.

### Get a king, then use it

A king can attack from behind, where your opponent's men can't defend. Bring it back into the game quickly.

## Other versions of draughts

- **International draughts** uses a 10 × 10 board and 20 pieces each. Men can capture backwards, and kings are "flying", which means they move any distance along a diagonal. Players must make the capture that takes the most pieces.
- **Russian and Brazilian draughts** use an 8 × 8 board with flying kings.
- **American checkers** follows the same rules as English draughts.
