Snakes and Ladders is one of the simplest board games ever made, and that's exactly why it's lasted for centuries. There are no decisions to make: roll the die, move your counter and hope for ladders. It's the perfect first game for young children.

## A game with ancient roots

The game comes from ancient India, where it was called **Moksha Patam** or **Gyan Chaupar**. It was used to teach moral lessons: ladders stood for good deeds that lifted you toward enlightenment, and snakes for bad deeds that dragged you back down. Old boards often had more snakes than ladders, a reminder that the path to virtue is hard.

The game reached England in the 1890s. In 1943, Milton Bradley released an American version called **Chutes and Ladders**, with slides instead of snakes.

## What you need

- A board of **100 numbered squares**, usually 10 rows of 10, numbered in a zigzag from the bottom-left corner
- One **die**
- A **counter** for each player

## How to play

1. Everyone rolls the die. The highest roller goes first.
2. All counters start off the board. Roll and move forward from square 1.
3. If you land at the **bottom of a ladder**, climb up to the square at the top.
4. If you land on the **head of a snake**, slide down to the square at its tail.
5. The first player to reach **square 100** wins.

## Popular rule variations

- **Exact finish:** you must roll the exact number to land on 100. If you roll too high, you stay put, or in some versions you bounce back by the extra amount.
- **Roll a six to start:** players need a six to enter the board.
- **Six means roll again:** rolling a six gives you another turn. Three sixes in a row sends you back to the start.
- **Two dice:** use two dice for a faster game with older children.
- **Snakes go up too:** in some homemade versions, landing on a snake's tail takes you up to its head, for an extra twist.

## Why it's great for kids

Snakes and Ladders looks simple, but it teaches young children some useful things:

- **Counting and number recognition** from 1 to 100
- **Taking turns** and following rules
- **Winning and losing gracefully**, because nobody can blame strategy when they slide down the big snake on square 98

## Tips for playing with young children

- Let them count the squares out loud as they move.
- Keep games short with a smaller board, or use the "no exact finish" rule.
- Make up stories about why each snake bites and where each ladder goes.

## Make your own board

Draw a 10 × 10 grid on a large sheet of paper, number it in a zigzag, and let your kids draw the snakes and ladders. Painted versions on school playgrounds are a great way to play the game outdoors with real jumping.
